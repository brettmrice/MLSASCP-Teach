Thrombophilia and Fibrinolysis

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate between hemostatic balance, thrombosis, and embolism, identifying the clinical and morphological features of arterial versus venous thrombi.
  • Characterize the pathophysiology, clinical consequences, and diagnostic testing of natural anticoagulant deficiencies (Antithrombin, Protein C, and Protein S).
  • Explain the molecular mechanisms and clinical significance of prothrombotic mutations, focusing on Factor V Leiden and Prothrombin G20210A.
  • Analyze the mechanisms of fibrinolytic system dysregulation, including plasminogen deficiency, tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) suppression, and plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI-1) excess.
  • Evaluate acquired thrombophilic conditions, detailing the laboratory diagnostic algorithm for Antiphospholipid Syndrome (Lupus Anticoagulant).
  • Compare anticoagulant and thrombolytic therapies, including their mechanisms of action, pre-analytical variables, and clinical laboratory monitoring protocols.

Hemostatic Balance, Thrombi Classification, and Embolism

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Hemostasis requires a constant, highly regulated equilibrium between procoagulant mechanisms (which drive fibrin generation) and anticoagulant systems (which limit clot propagation and degrade fibrin networks).

  • Primary Hemostasis: Yields a temporary, reversible platelet plug at the site of endothelial disruption.
  • Secondary Hemostasis: Generates a cross-linked fibrin matrix that stabilizes the primary aggregate.
  • Hypercoagulable State (Thrombophilia): Occurs when the hemostatic balance tips toward fibrin generation via excess procoagulant proteins, deficient natural coagulation inhibitors, or defective fibrinolysis.

A key mechanical and pathological distinction exists between physiological clots, thrombi, and emboli:

  • Clot: A physiological extravascular or intravascular mass composed of platelets, fibrin, and trapped red blood cells (RBCs) formed directly in response to endothelial disruption.
  • Thrombus: An pathological, inappropriate intravascular mass formed without physical disruption or vascular rupture, driven instead by endothelial dysfunction, hemodynamic stasis, or hypercoagulability (Virchow's Triad).
  • Embolus: A fragment of a thrombus (or other matter) that detaches, travels through systemic or pulmonary circulation, and lodges in a distant downstream microvascular bed, causing tissue ischemia and infarction.

Thrombi are categorized based on their vascular location, shear rates, and cellular composition:

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) encompasses both deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). DVT typically originates in the deep veins of the lower extremities (e.g., popliteal, femoral, iliac veins), producing unilateral erythema, edema, and warmth. A critical complication of DVT is the detachment of an embolus that migrates through the inferior vena cava, passes through the right side of the heart, and occludes the pulmonary arterial tree (pulmonary embolism), leading to acute right ventricular failure and sudden collapse.

In primary and secondary hemostasis diagnostics:

  • Routine screening assays (PT, APTT) assess plasma clotting factors in platelet-poor plasma (PPP). They do not detect thrombophilia or predict the presence of white or red thrombi directly.
  • Fibrin degradation product (FDP) and D-dimer immunoassays evaluate ongoing fibrin turnover. While FDP detects fragments from both fibrinogen and cross-linked fibrin (fragments X, Y, D, and E), D-dimer specifically requires Factor XIIIa-mediated cross-linking of the fibrin gamma chains. Thus, D-dimer is an explicit marker of true intravascular fibrinolysis.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Arterial thrombi are "white," shear-dependent, platelet-rich structures treated with antiplatelet agents.
  • Venous thrombi are "red," stasis-dependent, RBC/fibrin-rich structures treated with anticoagulants.
  • D-dimer evaluates the breakdown of stable, Factor XIIIa-cross-linked fibrin. It possesses a high negative predictive value (>95%) for ruling out DVT and PE when values fall within normal limits.

Natural Anticoagulant Deficiencies: Antithrombin, Protein C, and Protein S

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Endogenous anticoagulants prevent unchecked propagation of the clotting cascade beyond the local site of injury. Inherited deficiencies in these natural regulatory proteins result in sustained, uncontrolled thrombin generation.

1. Antithrombin (AT) Deficiency

  • Physiological Role: A serine protease inhibitor (serpin) synthesized by the liver. It neutralizes thrombin (Factor IIa), Factor Xa, Factor IXa, Factor XIa, and Factor XIIa by forming stable, equimolar protease-inhibitor complexes.
  • Heparin Catalysis: Endothelial heparan sulfate glycosaminoglycans and exogenous heparin bind allosterically to AT, inducing a conformational change that increases the inhibitory rate over 1,000-fold.
  • Classification:
    • Type I (Quantitative): Parallel reduction in both AT antigen concentration and AT functional/inhibitory activity (<50% of normal).
    • Type II (Qualitative): Normal AT antigen levels alongside reduced functional activity, reflecting mutations within the reactive site or the heparin-binding domain.

2. Protein C (PC) Deficiency

  • Physiological Role: A vitamin K-dependent zymogen synthesized in the liver. When thrombin binds the endothelial receptor thrombomodulin, thrombin's procoagulant affinity for fibrinogen is lost. The thrombin-thrombomodulin complex cleaves Protein C into activated Protein C (APC). APC proteolytically destroys essential cofactors Factor Va and Factor VIIIa.
  • Thrombin Activatable Fibrinolysis Inhibitor (TAFI) Link: Unregulated excess thrombin can activate TAFI, which cleaves basic residues (lysines) from partially degraded fibrin, preventing tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and plasminogen binding, thereby suppressing fibrinolysis.
  • Classification:
    • Type I (Quantitative): Simultaneous reduction in functional activity and antigenic mass.
    • Type II (Qualitative): Normal immunological/antigen levels with subnormal anticoagulant enzymatic activity.

3. Protein S (PS) Deficiency

  • Physiological Role: A vitamin K-dependent glycoprotein cofactor that non-enzymatically accelerates APC cleavage of Factors Va and VIIIa on negatively charged phospholipid membranes.
  • Forms in Circulation:
    • Bound Form (~60–65%): Non-covalently complexed with the complement regulatory protein C4b-binding protein (C4b-BP). This form lacks anticoagulant cofactor activity.
    • Free Form (~35–40%): Unbound and functionally active. Only free PS acts as the cofactor for APC.
  • Classification:
    • Type I (Quantitative): Decreases in total PS antigen, free PS antigen, and functional PS activity.
    • Type II (Qualitative): Normal total and free PS antigen levels, but reduced functional PS activity.
    • Type III (Distributional): Normal total PS antigen, but reduced free PS antigen and functional PS activity (frequently driven by acute-phase elevations of C4b-BP).

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

  • Routine Clotting Screening: Uncomplicated, baseline inherited deficiencies of AT, Protein C, or Protein S do not prolong routine PT, INR, or APTT assays. The standard screening reagents supply exogenous phospholipid and activator in excess to drive rapid clot formation in vitro, which bypasses regulatory feedback loops.
  • Warfarin-Induced Skin Necrosis (Purpura Fulminans): Protein C has the shortest half-life (~6 hours) of all vitamin K-dependent factors (Factor VII: ~6 hours; Factor IX: ~24 hours; Factor X: ~36 hours; Factor II: ~60 hours).
    • Following oral warfarin administration, Protein C levels decrease significantly faster than procoagulant factors II, IX, and X.
    • In a patient with an underlying, unrecognized heterozygous Protein C deficiency, early warfarin therapy drops Protein C to near-zero levels while procoagulants remain active.
    • This creates a transient, extreme hypercoagulable window, precipitating microvascular thrombosis in post-capillary venules, cutaneous ischemia, and extensive skin necrosis (purpura fulminans). Co-administration of bridging parenteral anticoagulation (unfractionated or low molecular weight heparin) until the INR reaches therapeutic range prevents this complication.

  • Laboratory Testing Criteria: Chromogenic substrate assays quantify AT activity by challenging patient plasma with excess Factor Xa or thrombin in the presence of heparin; residual protease activity inversely correlates with patient AT concentration. Protein C activity is assessed chromogenically using venom activators (e.g., Agkistrodon contortrix venom), while free Protein S is measured via automated latex immunoturbidimetric assays.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Baseline AT, PC, and PS deficiencies yield normal routine PT and APTT screening profiles.
  • Protein C's rapid turnover (half-life of 6 hours) necessitates heparin bridging when starting warfarin to avert purpura fulminans / warfarin-induced skin necrosis.
  • Routine testing must evaluate free Protein S, as the C4b-BP-bound fraction is functionally inactive.

Prothrombotic Gene Mutations: Factor V Leiden and Prothrombin G20210A

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Hypercoagulability can stem from gain-of-function molecular alterations in the structural genes encoding coagulation proteins. The two most common inherited forms are Factor V Leiden and the Prothrombin G20210A mutation.

1. Activated Protein C (APC) Resistance and Factor V Leiden

  • Pathophysiological Mechanism: Normally, Activated Protein C degrades Factor Va by cleaving specific peptide bonds, primarily at Arg506.
  • The Mutation: Factor V Leiden involves a single point mutation in the F5 gene: a guanine-to-adenine substitution at nucleotide 1691 ($1691\text{G}\to\text{A}$), which causes an arginine-to-glutamine substitution at amino acid position 506 ($\text{Arg}506\text{Gln}$).
  • Consequence: The altered cleavage site renders Factor Va resistant to APC inactivation. Mutated Factor Va retains full procoagulant function in the prothrombinase complex, generating excess thrombin over an extended duration.

2. Prothrombin G20210A Mutation

  • Pathophysiological Mechanism: A transition mutation ($20210\text{G}\to\text{A}$) in the 3' untranslated region (UTR) of the prothrombin (F2) gene.
  • Consequence: This non-coding region mutation stabilizes prothrombin messenger RNA (mRNA), increasing ribosomal translation and hepatic synthesis of prothrombin (Factor II). Circulating Factor II antigen levels are elevated 20–30% above normal baseline values, amplifying downstream thrombin generation capacity.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

  • Laboratory Detection of APC Resistance:

    • Evaluated using a clot-based APTT screening assay run in the presence and absence of exogenous, purified APC.
    • The patient's plasma is diluted (typically 1:4 or 1:5) in Factor V-deficient substrate plasma to normalize for discrepancies in other clotting factors and eliminate heparin artifacts.

    • Calculation (APC Sensitivity Ratio):

      $$\text{APC Ratio} = \frac{\text{APTT with Exogenous APC (seconds)}}{\text{APTT without APC (seconds)}}$$

    • Interpretation: In normal individuals, the addition of APC prolongs the clotting time ($\text{APC Ratio} \ge 2.0$). In patients with Factor V Leiden, the mutated factor resists inactivation, leading to an abnormally short APTT in the presence of APC and a decreased ratio ($\text{APC Ratio} < 2.0$).

    • Molecular Diagnostic Confirmation:
    • Clot-based APC resistance abnormalities and suspected Prothrombin G20210A cases are definitively confirmed via DNA-based molecular testing.
    • Techniques include real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), melting curve analysis, or targeted restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) testing to identify heterozygosity versus homozygosity.
    • Routine PT and APTT screens remain normal in uncomplicated carriers of both mutations.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Factor V Leiden is an inherited resistance to APC inactivation caused by an $\text{Arg}506\text{Gln}$ missense mutation.
  • Prothrombin G20210A is a non-coding regulatory 3' UTR mutation that causes hyperprothrombinemia by altering mRNA stability.
  • Clot-based APC resistance screening employs an APTT ratio calculation ($\text{APTT}_{+\text{APC}} / \text{APTT}_{-\text{APC}}$), with ratios $<2.0$ reflexing to confirmatory real-time PCR.

Fibrinolytic System Dysregulation

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

The fibrinolytic system is the enzymatic counterbalance to clot formation. Its primary function is the localized conversion of the inactive proenzyme plasminogen into the broad-spectrum serine protease plasmin, which lyses insoluble cross-linked fibrin polymers.

Mechanisms that disrupt this clearance pathway promote fibrin accumulation:

  • Dysfibrinogenemia: Structural mutations in the fibrinogen chains produce altered fibrin polymers that retain normal clotting kinetics but lack accessibility to plasmin cleavage domains.
  • Plasminogen Deficiency:
    • Type I (True Deficiency): Reduced antigenic concentration and functional activity; clinically characterized by ligneous conjunctivitis (pseudomembranous mucosal plaques) and elevated thrombosis risk.
    • Type II (Dysplasminogenemia): Normal antigenic mass alongside defective proteolytic cleavage capacity.
  • Impaired tPA Synthesis or Release: Defective endothelial release of tPA prevents localized activation of plasminogen at the surface of the thrombus.
  • PAI-1 Excess: Overexpression of Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor-1 (elevated in metabolic syndrome, severe obesity, and acute-phase inflammatory states) binds and inhibits free tPA and urokinase (uPA), preventing the initial generation of active plasmin.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

  • Laboratory Markers of Impaired Fibrinolysis:
    • Patients with hypofibrinolytic states (e.g., plasminogen deficiency, elevated PAI-1) show low baseline plasma D-dimer values due to subnormal plasmin activity.
    • In contrast, acute thrombotic events trigger secondary fibrinolysis, driving D-dimer values above reference cutoffs.
  • Fibrinogen Methodologies (Clauss Assay vs. Antigenic):
    • Clauss Assay: A clot-based method measuring the clotting time of diluted patient plasma upon addition of high-concentration bovine thrombin. The resulting time is inversely proportional to functional fibrinogen concentration.
    • Antigenic Assay: Automated immunoturbidimetric or radial immunodiffusion systems that quantify total physical protein mass.
    • Diagnostic Differentiation: A markedly prolonged Clauss thrombin clotting time paired with a normal quantitative fibrinogen antigen confirms dysfibrinogenemia.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Plasminogen activation by tPA cleaves insoluble fibrin into D-dimer complexes.
  • Inadequate tPA release, elevated PAI-1, or plasminogen deficiency impairs fibrin degradation, leading to baseline D-dimer suppression and persistent thrombi.
  • Dysfibrinogenemia is diagnosed in the laboratory by demonstrating a discrepancy between low functional activity (Clauss assay) and a normal quantitative antigen immunoassay.

Acquired Thrombophilia and Antiphospholipid Syndrome

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Acquired thrombophilia develops secondary to systemic pathology, medications, or pathological autoantibodies:

  • Hyperestrogenism: Oral contraceptives and pregnancy stimulate hepatic procoagulant protein synthesis (Factors II, VII, VIII, X) while inducing acquired resistance to APC and reducing free Protein S.
  • Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemias (MAHAs): Microvascular endothelial damage precipitates generalized microthrombi formation, platelet consumption, and mechanical RBC shearing (schistocytes):
    • Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): Systemic, unconstrained thrombin and plasmin generation.
    • Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP): Severe deficiency of the vWF-cleaving protease ADAMTS13 ($<10\text{%}$), producing ultra-large vWF multimers that trigger systemic microvascular platelet aggregation.
    • Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS): Endothelial destruction mediated by Shiga toxin or complement dysregulation.
  • Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (MPNs): Polycythemia vera and essential thrombocythemia harboring the JAK2 V617F mutation cause hyperviscosity, thrombocytosis, and intrinsic activation of circulating platelets.

Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) and Lupus Anticoagulant (LA)

APS is the most common acquired thrombophilia. Patients develop autoantibodies against phospholipid-binding plasma proteins (primarily beta-2-glycoprotein I and prothrombin).

  • The Clinical-Laboratory Paradox:
    • In Vivo (Clinical Reality): Antiphospholipid antibodies bind phospholipid-protein complexes on endothelial cells and platelets, inducing cell activation and creating a prothrombotic state (DVT, PE, arterial stroke, recurrent early miscarriages).
    • In Vitro (Laboratory Artifact): Antiphospholipid antibodies bind to the reagent phospholipid surfaces utilized in standard clot-based assays (e.g., APTT reagents). By competing for and neutralizing these required catalytic platforms, they retard assembly of the tenase and prothrombinase complexes, causing prolonged in vitro clotting times.

Diagnostic Evaluation Details

  1. Prolongation of a Phospholipid-Sensitive Screening Assay: Clotting time prolongation demonstrated on an assay such as an APTT using low-phospholipid formulations or the Dilute Russell Viper Venom Test (DRVVT) Screen. The venom of Daboia russelii directly activates Factor X in the presence of calcium and phospholipids, bypassing the intrinsic and extrinsic cascades.
  2. Failure to Correct on 1:1 Mixing Studies (Inhibitor Proof): Patient plasma is mixed 1:1 with Pooled Normal Plasma (PNP).
    • A true factor deficiency (e.g., Factor VIII deficiency) provides $\ge 50\text{%}$ factor activity, completely correcting the prolonged clotting time back toward the reference interval.
    • A circulating inhibitor (such as an antiphospholipid antibody) neutralizes the reagent surfaces, so the 1:1 mixture remains prolonged on both immediate and post-incubation testing.

  3. Phospholipid Neutralization (Confirmation Stage): Excess exogenous phospholipid (e.g., bilayer liposomes or frozen-thawed platelet lysates) is added to the assay system (DRVVT Confirm). The excess reagent phospholipid neutralizes the patient's antiphospholipid antibodies, freeing the remaining phospholipids to support coagulation complex assembly.

    • Calculated Ratio:

      $$\text{DRVVT Normalized Ratio} = \frac{\left(\frac{\text{DRVVT Screen Patient}}{\text{DRVVT Screen Normal PNP}}\right)}{\left(\frac{\text{DRVVT Confirm Patient}}{\text{DRVVT Confirm Normal PNP}}\right)}$$

    • Interpretation: A ratio exceeding defined cutoffs (typically $>1.20$) demonstrates phospholipid dependence and confirms the presence of a Lupus Anticoagulant.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Antiphospholipid Syndrome is clinically prothrombotic, but causes prolonged clotting times in vitro due to reagent phospholipid neutralization.
  • Mixing studies differentiate factor deficiencies from inhibitors: deficiencies correct; antiphospholipid antibodies do not.
  • The DRVVT confirms the diagnosis when an abnormal screen corrects upon the addition of excess bilayer phospholipids.

Anticoagulant and Thrombolytic Therapies and Laboratory Monitoring

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Medical management of thrombophilia balances systemic thrombus prevention with the risk of therapeutic hemorrhage.

1. Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)

  • Structure: Heterogeneous glycosaminoglycan mixture (molecular weight range: 5,000–30,000 Da).
  • Mechanism: Chains containing a specific high-affinity pentasaccharide sequence bind Antithrombin, accelerating its inhibition of Factor Xa and thrombin. To inactivate thrombin, heparin must bridge both Antithrombin and thrombin simultaneously (requiring $\ge 18$ saccharide units).
  • Laboratory Monitoring: Monitored via the APTT (traditionally targeted to 1.5–2.5 times the patient's baseline / mean normal control time) or direct chromogenic anti-Factor Xa assays (therapeutic target: 0.3–0.7 IU/mL).

2. Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH)

  • Structure: Depolymerized short-chain fragments (mean molecular weight: 4,000–5,000 Da; e.g., enoxaparin).
  • Mechanism: The shorter chains are insufficient to form the ternary bridge over thrombin, resulting in preferential inactivation of Factor Xa over thrombin (anti-Xa to anti-IIa activity ratios between 3:1 and 4:1).
  • Laboratory Monitoring: Because LMWH has minor effects on the APTT, it cannot be monitored using this assay. Routine monitoring is typically unnecessary due to predictable pharmacokinetics, but when indicated (e.g., renal failure, pregnancy, severe obesity), a chromogenic anti-Factor Xa assay calibrated specifically for LMWH is required (therapeutic peak target for twice-daily dosing: 0.6–1.0 IU/mL).

3. Warfarin (Coumadin)

  • Mechanism: Inhibits Vitamin K Epoxide Reductase (VKOR), an enzyme required to regenerate the reduced hydroquinone form of vitamin K. This impairs post-translational gamma-carboxylation of glutamic acid residues on Factors II, VII, IX, and X, as well as regulatory proteins C and S. Without gamma-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla) domains, these proteins cannot bind calcium or coordinate onto negatively charged phospholipid surfaces.
  • Laboratory Monitoring: Monitored using the Prothrombin Time (PT) and reported as the International Normalized Ratio (INR).

$$\text{INR} = \left(\frac{\text{Patient PT (seconds)}}{\text{Mean Normal PT (seconds)}}\right)^{\text{ISI}}$$
  • International Sensitivity Index (ISI): A calibration value assigned to each commercial thromboplastin lot by comparison against the World Health Organization (WHO) primary international reference preparation. A lower ISI (approaching 1.0) reflects greater reagent sensitivity.

4. Thrombolytic (Fibrinolytic) Agents

  • Agents: Recombinant tissue plasminogen activators (rtPA, alteplase, tenecteplase).
  • Mechanism: Proteolytically cleave the Arg561-Val562 bond of plasminogen trapped within a thrombus, generating active plasmin to degrade the fibrin mesh.
  • Laboratory Effects: High-dose systemic fibrinolysis consumes fibrinogen and Factor V/VIII cofactors, prolonging the PT, APTT, and thrombin time, accompanied by a sharp elevation in plasma D-dimer.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

  • Antiplatelet Therapy Monitoring: Antiplatelet drugs (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel) target platelet cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) or ADP P2Y12 receptors. Because routine PT and APTT testing uses platelet-poor plasma, it is unaffected by antiplatelet therapy. Monitoring requires whole-blood assays, such as the Platelet Function Analyzer (PFA-100/200) or VerifyNow turbidimetric systems.

  • Pre-Analytical Triaging & Specimen Integrity in Coagulation:

    • Coagulation testing requires 3.2% (0.109 M) buffered sodium citrate collected at an exact 9:1 blood-to-anticoagulant ratio.

    • High Hematocrit Adjustment: If a patient's hematocrit exceeds 55%, the relative volume of plasma is reduced, leaving an excess of free unchelated citrate that binds reagent calcium and falsely prolongs clot times. The citrate anticoagulant volume must be adjusted using the standard formula:

      $$C = \left(1.85 \times 10^{-3}\right) \times (100 - \text{HCT}) \times V$$


      (where $C$ is the required mL of sodium citrate, $\text{HCT}$ is patient hematocrit in %, and $V$ is total blood volume collected in mL).

    • Platelet-Poor Plasma (PPP): Specimens for APTT/heparin monitoring and Lupus Anticoagulant testing must be centrifuged to yield PPP ($\text{platelet count} < 10 \times 10^3/\mu\text{L}$). Residual platelets can release Platelet Factor 4 (PF4), which neutralizes heparin in vitro, or shed membrane fragments that mask lupus anticoagulants.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Unfractionated heparin targets both IIa and Xa equally and is monitored by APTT or anti-Xa; LMWH targets Xa preferentially and requires an anti-Xa assay calibrated specifically for LMWH.
  • Warfarin therapy is monitored exclusively using the INR, which standardizes for thromboplastin reagent sensitivity differences via the lot-specific ISI.
  • In vitro APTT/PT tests cannot monitor antiplatelet therapy because they require platelet-poor plasma; whole blood platelet function systems (PFA/VerifyNow) must be used instead.
  • A hematocrit $>55\text{%}$ requires manual adjustment of the sodium citrate volume to prevent falsely prolonged clotting times.

ASCP MLS Content Guideline Alignment

Hematology

  • Hemostasis
    • Physiology
      • Natural anticoagulant pathways (antithrombin inhibition kinetics, protein C activation by thrombomodulin, protein S bound versus free forms)
      • Fibrinolytic cascade mechanics (tPA release, plasminogen-to-plasmin conversion, PAI-1 inhibition, cross-linked fibrin cleavage into D-dimer)
      • Platelet versus coagulation factor dominance in white (arterial) versus red (venous) thrombus development
    • Disease States
      • Hypercoagulable states and thrombophilia
        • Hereditary defects (Antithrombin deficiency Type I/II, Protein C and Protein S deficiencies, Factor V Leiden mutation, Prothrombin G20210A gene substitution)
        • Acquired disorders (Antiphospholipid Syndrome / Lupus Anticoagulant, Warfarin-Induced Skin Necrosis / Purpura Fulminans, DIC, TTP, MPN-associated thrombosis)
    • Laboratory Testing
      • Clot-based functional coagulation assays (PT/INR, APTT, mixing studies for factor deficiency versus circulating inhibitors)
      • Specialized thrombophilia testing (Chromogenic Antithrombin, Chromogenic Protein C, Free Protein S immunoturbidimetric, Clot-based APC resistance ratio)
      • Phospholipid neutralization procedures (DRVVT Screen, DRVVT Confirm, normalized ratio calculations)
      • Fibrinolysis assays (Clauss functional fibrinogen versus antigenic assays, quantitative D-dimer latex immunoassays)
      • Anticoagulant therapeutic monitoring (APTT therapeutic ranges for UFH, chromogenic anti-Factor Xa assays for UFH and LMWH, PT/INR calculations using reagent ISI)

Laboratory Operations

  • Quality Assessment & Pre-analytical Variables
    • Specimen rejection and integrity criteria: sodium citrate 9:1 blood-to-anticoagulant ratio, tube underfilling errors, vacuum draw mechanics
    • High hematocrit anticoagulant volume correction calculation: $C = (1.85 \times 10^{-3}) \times (100 - \text{HCT}) \times V$
    • Centrifugation protocol to generate platelet-poor plasma ($\text{platelet count} < 10 \times 10^3/\mu\text{L}$) to eliminate Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) neutralization of heparin and membrane phospholipid contamination in Lupus Anticoagulant assays
  • Instrumentation & Calculations
    • International Normalized Ratio (INR) derivation: $\text{INR} = (\text{Patient PT} / \text{Mean Normal PT})^{\text{ISI}}$
    • Dilute Russell Viper Venom Test normalized ratio calculations
    • APC sensitivity ratio calculation for Factor V Leiden screening: $\text{Ratio} = \text{APTT}_{+\text{APC}} / \text{APTT}_{-\text{APC}}$
