Primary Hemostasis Disorders

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate primary from secondary hemostatic disorders based on clinical presentation, screening laboratory panels, and physiological mechanisms.
  • Evaluate the operational principles, cartridge configurations, pre-analytical variables, and diagnostic patterns of the Platelet Function Assay (PFA).
  • Analyze light transmission platelet aggregometry (LTA) principles, differentiating biphasic versus monophasic response curves across classic agonists.
  • Formulate a systematic diagnostic workup for quantitative platelet disorders, distinguishing pseudo-thrombocytopenia from immune and hereditary thrombocytopenias.
  • Categorize qualitative platelet disorders by functional phase (adhesion, granule secretion, aggregation) and interpret laboratory panels distinguishing Bernard-Soulier syndrome, Glanzmann thrombasthenia, and storage pool diseases.

Hemostatic Phases & Initial Screening

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Hemostasis is divided into three coordinated phases: primary hemostasis, secondary hemostasis, and fibrinolysis.

  • Primary Hemostasis: Comprises the vascular endothelium, subendothelial collagen matrix, and platelets. Injury triggers immediate local vasoconstriction to decrease the lumen diameter, increase local shear rates, and facilitate platelet-vessel wall contact. Platelets adhere, activate, secrete dense and alpha granules, and aggregate to build an unstable, temporary platelet plug.
  • Secondary Hemostasis: The enzymatic coagulation cascade culminates in thrombin generation. Thrombin cleaves soluble plasma fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands. Transglutaminase (Factor XIIIa) stabilizes this meshwork via covalent cross-links. Primary hemostasis terminates with fibrinogen-dependent platelet cross-linking; the conversion of fibrinogen to cross-linked fibrin belongs to secondary hemostasis.
  • Fibrinolysis: Regulates clot propagation and clears the intravascular thrombus after vessel repair. Endothelial release of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) converts the zymogen plasminogen into the active serine protease plasmin, which lyses fibrin into fibrin degradation products (FDPs) and cross-linked D-dimers.
  • Dual-Function Factors: Von Willebrand Factor (vWF) is unique because it participates in both primary and secondary hemostasis. In primary hemostasis, vWF acts as a molecular tether between subendothelial collagen and the platelet surface glycoprotein Ib/IX/V (GP Ib/IX/V) complex under high shear stress conditions (arterioles and microvasculature). In secondary hemostasis, circulating vWF serves as the non-covalent carrier protein for procoagulant Factor VIII (FVIII), protecting FVIII from rapid proteolytic degradation by activated Protein C. Severe vWF deficiency accelerates FVIII clearance, potentially prolonging the Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT).

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Superficial, immediate, mucocutaneous bleeds (petechiae, epistaxis, gingival oozing) point to primary hemostasis disorders (platelets/vasculature).
  • Deep tissue hematomas, hemarthroses, and delayed post-surgical bleeding point to secondary coagulation factor deficiencies.
  • An initial screening triage containing a CBC (evaluating platelets against the reference range of 150 – 450 $\times 10^3$/µL), PT, and aPTT rapidly localizes the defect: normal PT and aPTT alongside mucocutaneous bleeding indicate an isolated primary hemostatic disorder.
  • Von Willebrand disease bridges both arms; while functionally a primary platelet adhesion disorder, severe quantitative absence of vWF triggers accelerated FVIII destruction, which can present with an isolated prolonged aPTT.

Platelet Function Assay (PFA)

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

The Platelet Function Assay (PFA) is an automated, in vitro test system designed to assess primary hemostasis under high shear conditions ($5,000\text{ to }6,000\text{ s}^{-1}$), simulating the physiological environment of an injured arteriole. The PFA serves as an objective replacement for the obsolete in vivo Duke or Ivy bleeding time tests.

  • Test Principle & Instrumentation: Citrated whole blood is placed into disposable test cartridges. Under constant negative vacuum pressure, blood is drawn from a sample reservoir through a microscopic capillary into a microscopic aperture (diameter approximately 147–150 µm) cut into a bioactive membrane. The membrane is coated with equine Type I collagen and supplemented with either Epinephrine (Col/EPI) or Adenosine Diphosphate (Col/ADP).
  • Physiological Cascade Inside the Cartridge: As whole blood accelerates through the microscopic aperture under high shear, plasma vWF undergoes conformational unfolding. This exposes binding domains for platelet membrane GP Ib/IX/V receptors, triggering adhesion. Platelets activate upon contact with the collagen matrix and biochemical agonist (epinephrine or ADP), recruit circulating platelets, and aggregate via GP IIb/IIIa–fibrinogen bridges. The growing platelet plug physically occludes the aperture. The diagnostic endpoint is the Closure Time (CT), recorded in seconds, which marks the exact moment platelet aggregation completely blocks blood flow.
  • Dual Cartridge Protocol: PFA analysis requires running both the Col/EPI and Col/ADP cartridges to differentiate intrinsic platelet receptor/pathway defects from extrinsic cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) pharmacotherapy (aspirin).

PFA results are sensitive to qualitative defects, plasma vWF levels, and specific pre-analytical parameters.

  • Strict Pre-Analytical Cutoffs:
    • Platelet Count Cutoff: The patient's automated platelet count must be $\ge 100 \times 10^3$/µL. Counts below this cutoff lead to artifactual prolongation of the closure time due to cell scarcity rather than functional platelet impairment.
    • Hematocrit (HCT) Cutoff: The patient's hematocrit must be $\ge 30\text{%}$ (reference range: 35 – 50%). Erythrocytes physically push platelets toward the vessel margin (platelet margination) and increase bulk blood viscosity. When HCT falls $< 30\text{%}$, the reduced blood viscosity decreases shear forces at the aperture margin, causing a false prolongation of closure times.
    • Specimen Requirements: Testing requires $3.2\text{%}$ (0.109 M) sodium citrate whole blood. Specimen testing must occur within 4 hours of collection. Samples must not be centrifuged (as primary hemostasis requires red cells and platelets together).

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Col/EPI prolonged + Col/ADP normal indicates an aspirin effect (irreversible platelet COX-1 inhibition).
  • Col/EPI prolonged + Col/ADP prolonged indicates an intrinsic primary hemostatic defect (vWD, Bernard-Soulier, Glanzmann) or severe storage pool disease, prompting reflex to light aggregometry.
  • Always review the baseline CBC before validating PFA results: a platelet count $< 100 \times 10^3$/µL or a hematocrit $< 30\text{%}$ causes artifactual closure time prolongations.
  • Aspirin affects platelets for their lifespan (7–10 days). Patients must discontinue aspirin therapy for at least 10–14 days before accurate baseline platelet function or closure testing can be achieved.

Light Transmission Platelet Aggregometry (LTA)

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Light Transmission Aggregometry (LTA), historically termed Born aggregometry, is the reference standard for investigating qualitative platelet disorders and platelet receptor/signaling dysfunctions.

  • Sample Preparation: Whole blood collected in $3.2\text{%}$ sodium citrate is separated via differential centrifugation:
    • Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP): Prepared by gentle centrifugation at low gravitational force ($150 \times g$ for 10–15 minutes) with the brake off. This leaves uninjured platelets suspended in native plasma. The platelet count in PRP is adjusted to approximately $200\text{ to }300 \times 10^3$/µL using autologous platelet-poor plasma.
    • Platelet-Poor Plasma (PPP): Prepared by high-speed centrifugation ($2000\text{ to }2500 \times g$ for 15 minutes). PPP must achieve an absolute platelet count of $< 10 \times 10^3$/µL (verified semi-annually during laboratory centrifuge speed quality control).
  • Instrument Calibration: In the aggregometer cuvette maintained at $37^\circ\text{C}$ with constant stirring:
    • PRP represents $0\text{%}$ Light Transmittance (maximal turbidity / minimal optical transmittance).
    • PPP represents $100\text{%}$ Light Transmittance (clear baseline).
  • Optical Principle: Following baseline stabilization, an aggregating agonist is introduced. Resting discoid platelets first undergo shape change (pseudopod formation), causing a slight transient increase in turbidity (a minor dip in light transmittance). As platelets aggregate into macro-aggregates, cleared plasma allows more light to reach the photodetector, generating an upward deflection in percent transmittance.
  • Biphasic vs. Monophasic Waves:
    • Primary Wave of Aggregation: Represents weak, reversible platelet-to-platelet adhesion directly induced by the exogenous agonist (e.g., low-dose ADP or epinephrine) without granule secretion.
    • Secondary Wave of Aggregation: Represents irreversible platelet clumping driven by the autocrine and paracrine release of endogenous substances from platelet storage granules (ADP, serotonin) and endogenous synthesis of thromboxane $\text{A}_2$ ($\text{TXA}_2$).

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

The specific agonists used in LTA challenge distinct receptors, metabolic pathways, and storage pools:

  • Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP): At physiological concentrations ($2\text{ to }5\text{ }\mu\text{M}$), produces a classic biphasic aggregation curve. The primary wave reflects direct stimulation of platelet purinergic receptors ($\text{P2Y}_1$ and $\text{P2Y}_{12}$). The secondary wave requires endogenous dense granule release. Aspirin-treated platelets or storage pool deficiencies fail to produce the secondary wave.
  • Epinephrine: Binds platelet $\alpha_2$-adrenergic receptors. Generates a distinct biphasic wave. Highly sensitive to cyclooxygenase inhibition and storage pool defects.
  • Collagen: Displays a characteristic lag phase (lasting 1–2 minutes) during which platelets must first adhere to collagen fibers via GP VI and $\alpha_2\beta_1$ receptors and synthesize $\text{TXA}_2$. Once activated, collagen produces a direct, steep monophasic curve reflecting granule release. It lacks an isolated primary wave.
  • Arachidonic Acid: Directly evaluates the platelet cyclooxygenase pathway. The enzyme COX-1 converts arachidonic acid to prostaglandin $\text{H}_2$ ($\text{PGH}_2$), which is metabolized by thromboxane synthase into $\text{TXA}_2$. Arachidonic acid produces a single monophasic wave. It shows absent aggregation in patients taking aspirin, NSAIDs, or those with congenital COX-1 deficiency.
  • Ristocetin: An antibiotic that induces conformational changes in plasma vWF, forcing it to bind the platelet GP Ib/IX/V receptor complex. Ristocetin-induced aggregation (agglutination) does not require active metabolic platelet aggregation; it tests the integrity of the GP Ib/IX/V-vWF axis:
    • Ristocetin produces a monophasic or biphasic agglutination curve.
    • Differentiation with Normal Plasma: If ristocetin agglutination is absent, the scientist can run a mixing correction test:
    • Mixing patient PRP with normal pooled plasma supplies wild-type vWF. If agglutination is restored, the defect is von Willebrand Disease.
    • If agglutination remains absent despite the addition of normal plasma, the defect is Bernard-Soulier Syndrome (congenital absence of the platelet GP Ib/IX/V receptor).

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Bernard-Soulier Syndrome: Impaired ristocetin agglutination; normal aggregation with ADP, epinephrine, collagen, and arachidonic acid. Adding normal plasma fails to correct the ristocetin response (absent GP Ib receptors).
  • von Willebrand Disease: Impaired ristocetin agglutination; normal aggregation with ADP, epinephrine, collagen, and arachidonic acid. Adding normal plasma corrects the ristocetin response (replaces deficient plasma vWF).
  • Glanzmann Thrombasthenia: Impaired aggregation across ADP, epinephrine, collagen, and arachidonic acid; normal agglutination with ristocetin.
  • Aspirin Therapy / Storage Pool Defects: Absent secondary aggregation wave with low-dose ADP and epinephrine; absent aggregation with arachidonic acid.
  • Pre-analytical validation requires verifying that the PPP has $< 10 \times 10^3$ platelets/µL and the adjusted PRP has $200\text{ to }300 \times 10^3$ platelets/µL before recording LTA traces.

Quantitative Platelet Disorders

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Quantitative disorders involve alterations in circulating platelet mass, defined as either thrombocytopenia (platelet count $< 150 \times 10^3$/µL) or thrombocytosis (platelet count $> 450 \times 10^3$/µL).

  • Pseudo-thrombocytopenia (In Vitro Pre-Analytical Artifact):

    • Mechanism: Triggered by an in vitro EDTA-dependent IgG or IgM autoantibody directed against hidden (cryptic) epitopes on the platelet GP IIb/IIIa complex. When whole blood is collected into potassium $\text{EDTA}$ tubes ($\text{K}_2\text{EDTA}$ or $\text{K}_3\text{EDTA}$), the calcium-chelating agent alters the spatial conformation of GP IIb/IIIa, exposing these cryptic epitopes.
    • Microscopic Presentation: The bound autoantibodies cause platelets to aggregate into large masses or adhere to neutrophil membranes (platelet satellitosis).
    • Instrumentation Error: Automated hematology analyzers using impedance or optical light scatter count these platelet clumps as single leukocytes (small lymphocytes), generating a falsely low platelet count and a falsely elevated white blood cell count.
    • Resolution Protocol: The laboratory professional must verify the smear, reject the EDTA platelet value, and request a fresh redraw in a $3.2\text{%}$ sodium citrate (light-blue top) tube. Because sodium citrate contains a liquid anticoagulant that dilutes blood by $1:9$, the raw platelet count from the analyzer must be multiplied by a factor of $1.1$:

      $$\text{Corrected Platelet Count} = \text{Citrate Tube Platelet Count} \times 1.1$$

  • Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP):

    • Pathophysiology: Characterized by circulating autoantibodies (most commonly IgG) directed against platelet surface glycoproteins (GP IIb/IIIa or GP Ib/IX/V).
    • Clearance Mechanism: Antibody-coated platelets are recognized by Fc$\gamma$ receptors on splenic macrophages within the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and prematurely destroyed.
    • Clinical Profiles: Pediatric ITP often manifests acutely following an intense viral infection (cross-reactive post-viral molecular mimicry). Adult ITP is typically chronic and insidious, affecting women more frequently than men.
    • Laboratory Findings: Severe, isolated thrombocytopenia (often $< 20 \times 10^3$/µL in acute phases); normal PT, INR, and aPTT; bone marrow megakaryocytic hyperplasia (compensatory platelet production); peripheral smear shows normal erythrocyte/leukocyte morphologies and occasional stress platelets (megathrombocytes).
  • Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT Type II):
    • Pathophysiology: A life-threatening immune complication occurring 5–10 days following unfractionated heparin (or low-molecular-weight heparin) therapy.
    • Mechanism: Patients form an IgG antibody directed against neo-epitopes on multimolecular complexes of positively charged Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) and negatively charged Heparin. The resulting HIT-IgG/PF4/Heparin immune complexes cross-link platelet Fc$\gamma$RIIa receptors.
    • Paradoxical Thrombosis: This receptor engagement triggers widespread platelet activation, procoagulant microparticle shedding, and high thrombin generation. Consequently, HIT presents with consumption thrombocytopenia alongside life-threatening arterial and venous thrombosis (Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia and Thrombosis / HITT).
    • Diagnostic Criteria: A drop in the automated platelet count of $\ge 50\text{%}$ from baseline (even if the absolute platelet count remains $> 150 \times 10^3$/µL). Screening requires an anti-PF4/heparin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), confirmed by a functional Serotonin Release Assay (SRA).
  • Hereditary Thrombocytopenias:
    • May-Hegglin Anomaly: Autosomal dominant mutation in the MYH9 gene encoding non-muscle myosin heavy chain IIA. Laboratory findings feature mild-to-moderate thrombocytopenia, prominent giant platelets (often the diameter of erythrocytes), and pale-blue, spindle-shaped Döhle-like inclusion bodies composed of precipitated myosin within the cytoplasm of neutrophils and monocytes. Patients have normal leukocyte function and minimal bleeding symptoms.
    • Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome (WAS): X-linked recessive mutation in the WAS gene. Triad consists of micro-thrombocytopenia (exceptionally small platelets with decreased mean platelet volume, MCV $< 5\text{ fL}$), severe recurrent pyogenic infections (immunodeficiency), and intractable eczema.
    • Bernard-Soulier Syndrome: Autosomal recessive deficiency in GP Ib/IX/V. Presents with moderate thrombocytopenia, circulating giant platelets, and abnormal platelet adhesion.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Pseudo-thrombocytopenia Triad: Analyzer low platelet flag + automated WBC elevation + peripheral smear clumps/satellitosis. Correct by redrawing in a sodium citrate tube and applying the $1.1\times$ multiplication factor.
  • Platelet Size Differentiation:
    • Giant Platelets: May-Hegglin anomaly (paired with neutrophil Döhle-like bodies) and Bernard-Soulier syndrome.
    • Micro-platelets: Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (triad: micro-thrombocytopenia, eczema, recurrent infections).
  • The HIT Principle: Suspect HIT if a patient on heparin displays a $\ge 50\text{%}$ drop in platelet count. HIT causes thrombosis through platelet-activating immune complexes (IgG anti-PF4/heparin binding Fc$\gamma$RIIa receptors).
  • Essential Thrombocythemia: A paradoxical myeloproliferative state where the absolute platelet count is markedly elevated ($> 450 \times 10^3$/µL, often $> 1,000 \times 10^3$/µL), yet the patient exhibits mucocutaneous bleeding due to structural platelet dysfunction or acquired vWS.

  • Adhesion Phase Disorders:
    • Bernard-Soulier Syndrome (BSS):
    • Molecular Etiology: Autosomal recessive deficiency or qualitative defect in the Glycoprotein Ib/IX/V complex (subunits GP Ib$\alpha$ [CD42b], GP Ib$\beta$ [CD42c], GP IX [CD42a], and GP V [CD42d]).
    • Physiological Defect: Platelets cannot bind subendothelial-bound vWF under high arterial shear stress, preventing primary platelet adhesion.
    • Morphology & Laboratory Profile: Thrombocytopenia (variable, usually $20\text{ to }100 \times 10^3$/µL); characteristic giant platelets ($3.5\text{ to }8\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ in diameter); prolonged PFA closure times (Col/EPI and Col/ADP); LTA shows normal aggregation with ADP, epinephrine, and collagen, but completely absent agglutination with ristocetin that fails to correct with normal pooled plasma; flow cytometry confirms marked reduction or complete absence of CD42b / CD42a.
  • Granule & Secretion Phase Disorders:
    • Alpha-Storage Pool Deficiency (Gray Platelet Syndrome):
    • Molecular Etiology: Autosomal recessive mutation in the NBEAL2 gene, leading to the failure to package proteins into alpha granules during megakaryocyte maturation. Platelets contain a normal dense granule count (5–8 per platelet) but completely lack alpha granules (normally 50–80 per platelet).
    • Morphology & Laboratory Profile: Mild thrombocytopenia; platelets appear distinctively enlarged, pale, gray, and ghost-like/hypogranular on Wright-Giemsa stained peripheral smears. Mild bleeding diathesis; impaired aggregation to thrombin and collagen; progressive marrow myelofibrosis due to premature leakage of PDGF into the marrow space.
    • Dense-Storage Pool Deficiencies ($\delta$-Storage Pool Disease):
    • Molecular Etiology: Failure to form or store platelet dense granules, depleting the storage pool of SACRED contents: Serotonin, Adenosine triphosphate (ATP), Calcium, and Releasable Endogenous DP.
    • Clinical Syndromes: Found in isolation or associated with systemic autosomal recessive albinism disorders:
      • Hermansky-Pudlak Syndrome: Tyrosinase-positive oculocutaneous albinism, dense granule absence, and lysosomal ceroid lipofuscin accumulation leading to pulmonary fibrosis.
      • Chediak-Higashi Syndrome: Mutation in LYST gene; displays oculocutaneous albinism, severe immunodeficiency, recurrent pyogenic infections, dense granule deficiency, and diagnostic giant lysosomal azurophilic granules in neutrophils and lymphocytes on the peripheral smear.
    • Aggregometry Findings: Normal primary wave of aggregation with low-dose ADP and epinephrine; absent secondary aggregation wave (due to failure to release endogenous storage ADP); absent aggregation with collagen.
  • Aggregation Phase Disorders:
    • Glanzmann Thrombasthenia (GT):
    • Molecular Etiology: Autosomal recessive deficiency or qualitative dysfunction of Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (integrin $\alpha_{\text{IIb}}\beta_3$, composed of CD41 and CD61).
    • Physiological Defect: While platelets adhere to the damaged vessel wall and activate normally, they cannot bind soluble divalent plasma fibrinogen to cross-link adjacent platelets together. Aggregation is entirely blocked.
    • Morphology & Laboratory Profile: Normal platelet count; completely normal platelet morphology (normal size and granulation); severely prolonged PFA closure times ($> 300\text{ s}$ on both cartridges); LTA shows absent aggregation to ADP, epinephrine, collagen, and arachidonic acid, but completely intact, normal agglutination with ristocetin; flow cytometry confirms absence or severe reduction of CD41 (GP IIb) and CD61 (GP IIIa).
  • Acquired Qualitative Defect — Cyclooxygenase Inhibition (Aspirin):
    • Molecular Mechanism: Aspirin acetylates a serine residue (Ser-529) in the active site of the platelet cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) enzyme.
    • Downstream Signaling Failure: This blocks the conversion of membrane-derived arachidonic acid into prostaglandin $\text{H}_2$ ($\text{PGH}_2$), preventing the generation of Thromboxane $\text{A}_2$ ($\text{TXA}_2$). Without $\text{TXA}_2$, platelets cannot mobilize intracellular calcium or undergo second-wave autocrine granule secretion. Because platelets lack nuclei and ribosomal machinery, they cannot synthesize new COX-1; inhibition persists for the platelet lifespan (7–10 days).
    • Laboratory Profile: Normal platelet count; prolonged PFA Col/EPI closure time with normal Col/ADP closure time; LTA demonstrates normal primary aggregation wave with low-dose ADP, absent secondary wave with low-dose ADP/epinephrine, and complete absence of aggregation with arachidonic acid.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Flow cytometry is the confirmatory test for establishing specific platelet glycoprotein deficiencies. Monoclonal antibodies targeting cluster of differentiation (CD) markers confirm diagnostic membrane profiles.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Bernard-Soulier vs. Glanzmann Distinction:
    • Bernard-Soulier Syndrome: Giant platelets, mild thrombocytopenia, absent LTA response to ristocetin, normal response to ADP/epinephrine/collagen. Surface markers: deficient CD42b / CD42a.
    • Glanzmann Thrombasthenia: Normal platelet count, normal morphology, absent LTA response to ADP/epinephrine/collagen, normal response to ristocetin. Surface markers: deficient CD41 / CD61.
  • Gray Platelet Syndrome Smear Identification: Platelets appear completely devoid of pink-purple granules, presenting as pale gray, ghost-like discs due to congenital absence of alpha granules (NBEAL2 mutation).
  • Hermansky-Pudlak & Chediak-Higashi Associations: Oculocutaneous albinism with bleeding symptoms indicates dense storage pool disease. Chediak-Higashi is distinguished on the blood smear by giant lysosomal granules in neutrophils.
  • Aspirin Aggregation Diagnostic: Complete failure to aggregate when exposed to exogenous arachidonic acid, accompanied by the loss of the secondary aggregation wave with low-dose ADP.

Hematology

  • Physiology
    • Hemostasis Core Mechanics
      • Vascular system role: Local vasoconstriction and endothelial high-shear interactions
      • Primary vs. secondary hemostasis differentiation: Platelet plug vs. fibrin formation
      • Fibrinolytic system activation: Tissue plasminogen activator, plasminogen conversion, and plasmin activity
      • Vitamin K-dependent coagulation proteins: Factors II, VII, IX, X, Protein C, and Protein S
      • Contact activation and intrinsic pathway factors: Factors XII, XI, IX, and VIII
      • Extrinsic and common coagulation factor cascades: Factors VII, X, V, II, and I
      • Fibrin stabilization: Transglutaminase Factor XIII activation
      • Dual hemostatic functions of von Willebrand factor: Carrier for Factor VIII and high-shear platelet adhesion bridge
  • Disease States
    • Quantitative Platelet Disorders
      • Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP): Clinical pathophysiology, viral mimicry, and bone marrow megakaryopoiesis
      • Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT): IgG anti-PF4/heparin complexes, Fc$\gamma$RIIa binding, and consumption with paradoxical thrombosis
      • Hereditary thrombocytopenias: May-Hegglin anomaly (giant platelets and Döhle-like leukocyte inclusions), Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (micro-thrombocytopenia, eczema, immunodeficiency)
      • Clonal thrombocythemia vs. reactive thrombocytosis: Myeloproliferative neoplasms (ET, PV, CML) vs. acute phase reactions and post-splenectomy
    • Qualitative Platelet Disorders
      • Adhesion defect: Bernard-Soulier syndrome (GP Ib/IX/V complex deficiency, giant platelets, and high-shear adhesion failure)
      • Aggregation defect: Glanzmann thrombasthenia (GP IIb/IIIa integrin deficiency, fibrinogen cross-linking failure)
      • Granule deficiencies: Gray platelet syndrome (alpha-granule absence, pale gray smear morphology), dense-granule deficiencies (Hermansky-Pudlak, Chediak-Higashi syndrome)
      • Drug-induced platelet dysfunction: Irreversible cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) inhibition by aspirin, prevention of thromboxane $\text{A}_2$ synthesis
  • Laboratory Testing
    • Primary Hemostasis Testing
      • Complete blood count (CBC): Reference interval $150\text{ to }450 \times 10^3$/µL, smear verification, and stress platelets
      • Platelet Function Assay (PFA): High shear aperture mechanics ($5,000\text{ to }6,000\text{ s}^{-1}$), cartridge agonist configurations (Col/EPI and Col/ADP), pre-analytical cutoffs ($\text{Platelet Count} \ge 100 \times 10^3$/µL, $\text{HCT} \ge 30\text{%}$), and pattern interpretation
      • Light Transmission Aggregometry (LTA): Platelet-rich plasma ($200\text{ to }300 \times 10^3$/µL) vs. platelet-poor plasma ($< 10 \times 10^3$/µL), optical transmittance principles, biphasic vs. monophasic curves, and agonist profiling (ADP, Epinephrine, Collagen, Arachidonic Acid, Ristocetin)
      • Ristocetin agglutination and normal plasma mixing correction studies for differentiating BSS from vWD
      • Flow cytometry immunophenotyping: CD42b/CD42a gating for GP Ib/IX/V in Bernard-Soulier syndrome; CD41/CD61 gating for GP IIb/IIIa in Glanzmann thrombasthenia
    • Secondary Hemostasis Screening
      • Routine coagulation screening: Prothrombin Time / International Normalized Ratio (PT/INR) and Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT)
      • Plasma specimen validation: Platelet-poor plasma centrifugation validation criteria ($< 10 \times 10^3$/µL)
